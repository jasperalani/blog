You're on a country road with one lane in each direction. The limit is 60 mph, the road ahead is clear, and you're making good progress. Then you come round a bend and there it is: a line of cars, all doing 40, all following one driver at the front who is perfectly happy at that speed. You ease off, join the back, and become part of the line.

Most of us have sat in that line more times than we could count. What caught my attention is what it would look like from above. If you could watch a stretch of road like this from the air, you wouldn't see cars spread evenly along it. You'd see groups. One group here, then another further down the road, each one sitting behind a single car that doesn't want to go fast.

You only need one simple rule to explain that pattern.

## The one rule

On a single-lane road, a car that wants to go fast goes fast. If there's no car in front, it just goes. But if there's another car in front that doesn't want to go fast, it has to wait behind it. It has to sit behind.

That's really all there is to it. Every driver has a speed they'd like to do, and they do that speed until they catch up with someone slower. From then on, they do the slower driver's speed instead. The faster driver hasn't done anything wrong. They've simply run out of empty road.

## How the groups form

Now put a steady stream of cars on the same road. Some drivers want to go at close to the limit, and some are happy a good deal slower. In the animation below, roughly a third of drivers are in the slower camp.

At the start of the road the cars are spread out more or less at random, because that's how they happened to arrive. But every fast driver is gaining on whoever is in front. Sooner or later they catch up with a slower car, and once they do, they're stuck. Then the next fast driver catches up with them, and so on down the line.

So the further along the road you look, the more bunched up the traffic becomes. You end up with groups of cars moving together, each one led by a slow driver. Behind that leader you'll get a mix: some cars that do want to go fast, and some that don't. A slow driver who comes up behind an even slower one gets stuck too.

Traffic engineers have a name for these groups. They call them platoons.

What I like about this is that nobody in the platoon chose to be there. Nobody organised it either. It's simply what you get when lots of people follow the same rule on a road where there's only one lane to share.

## What breaks a group up

A group keeps moving like this until something changes at the front. On most country roads, that something is a turn-off. The leader indicates, slows down, and turns into a side road, a farm track, or a driveway.

What happens next depends on who was sitting directly behind them.

If the next car is a fast driver, they speed up and pull away. So does the fast driver behind them. The front of the group spaces out, and for a while it looks like ordinary free-flowing traffic again.

But if the car behind the one that just turned off also wants to go slow, not much changes. That driver becomes the new leader, everyone behind them stays where they are, and the group keeps moving, just with a different car at the front.

In practice you often see both at once. The fast drivers who were directly behind the old leader break away, and the rest of the group carries on behind the next slow driver. That's what happens at the side road in the animation above.

## Why this matters

The key point here is that on these roads, your speed isn't really set by you. It's set by the driver at the front of your group. You can want to do 55 mph all you like, but if the leader wants to do 40, you're doing 40.

It also means one slow driver affects everyone behind them, not just the car directly behind. The delay doesn't fall on one person. It's shared out along the whole line, and the longer the line, the more time is lost in total.

This matters because it explains why the queue doesn't sort itself out. Driving closer to the car in front doesn't help, because the whole group can only go as fast as its leader. A group only breaks up when the leader leaves the road, or when drivers overtake.

The animation deliberately leaves overtaking out. On a real road you can overtake where it's safe and legal, but on a busy, twisting road with oncoming traffic those chances can be rare, which is exactly why groups last as long as they do.

None of this is a complaint about slow drivers. Forty on a narrow country road is often perfectly sensible, and a speed limit is a maximum rather than a target. The Highway Code does have some advice for the driver at the front, though.

<Callout title="What the Highway Code says">
  Rule 169: "Do not hold up a long queue of traffic, especially if you are driving a large or slow-moving vehicle. Check your mirrors frequently, and if necessary, pull in where it is safe and let traffic pass." You can read it in full in [the Highway Code on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-highway-code/using-the-road-159-to-203).
</Callout>

In a way, pulling in now and then is the slow driver's own version of a turn-off. It does the same job: it lets the faster drivers behind get away.

Next time you're sitting in one of these groups, have a look at who's directly behind the leader. If they're a fast driver, you'll be on your way the moment the leader turns off. If they're slow, settle in.
